Lottery Edge Lab · built for Sovereignty

Can AI beat the lottery? No.

Every combination of numbers is exactly as likely as every other, and both games pay only half of what they take in back out as prizes. We went looking for an edge anyway, using ticket sales, taxes, the odds of every prize tier and the prize-winner counts from 2,327 Powerball and Mega Millions draws. Nothing makes a ticket worth its price. Three levers make it lose less, and one of them is a real advantage over other players.

Tool 2 · the optimal spread

Spreading several tickets

Your questionIf you play several games, is there an optimal spread of numbers?

There is, and here is exactly what it buys you. Any set of different tickets has the same jackpot odds: 10 tickets are 10 chances in 292,201,338 however you arrange them, and the average payout is fixed too. The arrangement controls two other things: how often you win something, and how many people you'd share a jackpot with.

Game
Style
  • A different bonus ball on every ticket until all are used. The bonus ball decides most small prizes.
  • No two tickets share more than one white ball, so they never win or lose as a block.
  • White balls mostly from the underplayed range, and the least-picked bonus balls first.
  • No runs, evenly spaced sets, same last digits, famous combos or past winners.

      Evidence 1 · player habits

      We measured which numbers people pick

      Your questionDo people who choose their own numbers lean toward certain numbers, and can public data show it?

      Yes, clearly. After every draw, the lotteries publish how many tickets won each of the nine prize tiers. When the winning numbers are popular, more tickets match them. We collected those counts for 1,396 Powerball draws (October 2015 to September 2026) and 931 Mega Millions draws (October 2017 to September 2026), then fitted a model of how people pick their numbers.

      What the data says

      • 7 is the most over-picked number in both games. It appears on about 1.66× as many self-picked tickets as the average number. Then come 11, 9, 3 and 12.
      • Birthdays dominate. Numbers 1 to 31 run about 1.29× average as a block, and 32 to 69 about 0.76×. The least-picked numbers, 64, 61 and 63, sit under 0.6×.
      • The red ball follows the same habits. Powerball 7 is picked 1.43× as often as average, while 1, 16 and 20 are near 0.78×. Mega Millions players favor Mega Ball 11 and 7.
      • The model checks out. From winner counts alone, it reproduces each draw's reported ticket sales to within about 1% (median over 1,370 Powerball and 911 Mega Millions draws).
      • Patterns are far worse than any single number. In Dutch lottery data, 1-2-3-4-5-6 was on about 3,100× as many tickets as a random combination, and the “Lost” numbers on about 5,100× (Wang et al., 2016).
      • The exact multipliers depend on the quick-pick share. Lottery officials put it near 70%. At 60% or 80%, the 7 comes out at 1.5× or 1.95×.

      Past draws predict nothing

      The other hope was that AI could find patterns in the winning numbers themselves. We tested every Powerball draw since its current 69-ball format began in October 2015, and every Mega Millions draw since its 70-ball format began in October 2017.

      Hot numbers don't stay hot, and cold numbers aren't due. Across 1,314 test draws, betting on the recent favorites or the recent laggards landed within about one standard error (±0.02) of chance. Numbers from the previous draw came back 0.358 times per draw, against 0.362 expected. Mega Millions shows the same thing.

      The draws are physical and supervised. Powerball uses ball machines with eight ball sets at the Florida Lottery studio in Tallahassee, observed by an independent accounting firm. Mega Millions uses machines at WSB-TV in Atlanta under Georgia Lottery supervision. Every known rigged draw was rigged by people: latex-injected balls in Pennsylvania's 1980 “Triple Six Fix”, or a lottery insider's hidden code in the Eddie Tipton case. None was caught by a frequency chart.

      One of our checks did come out borderline. Powerball numbers that were frequent in the first half of the data were slightly more frequent in the second half too (correlation 0.26, p = 0.03). Split the draws any other way, odd against even or year by year, and it fades. The same test on Mega Millions finds nothing (p = 0.36). Run a dozen tests on random data and one of them usually looks like this. That is how “AI finds a pattern in the lottery” stories get made.

      History

      People who really beat a lottery

      Real edges have existed. Every one came from a flaw in one particular game, and every one got closed.

      1992

      Stefan Mandel buys up VirginiaMath

      Virginia's lotto had 7,059,052 combinations at $1 and a record jackpot of about $27M. Mandel's fund of about 2,500 investors pre-printed play slips in Australia and bought millions of tickets, around 5 million by 1992 accounts. It held the only winning ticket.

      Closed byRules against bulk buying, and games far too big to cover. Every Powerball combination would cost $584M.
      1992

      An Irish syndicate buys 80% of the LottoMath

      Ireland's 6-from-36 game had 1,947,792 combinations. A 28-member syndicate bought over 80% of them and held the winning line for the IR£1.7M jackpot. Two other tickets won too, so it kept a third, and lower prizes lifted its profit to about IR£310,000.

      Closed byA switch to 6-from-39 three months later.
      2003

      Mohan Srivastava reads scratch ticketsGame flaw

      A Toronto geostatistician noticed that the visible numbers on Ontario's Tic-Tac-Toe scratch ticket gave away winners. He sorted 20 unscratched tickets and called 19 correctly, then reported it instead of cashing in.

      Closed byThe lottery pulling the game.
      2004–12

      Cash WinFall's roll-down loopholeMath

      When the Massachusetts jackpot passed $2M without a winner, the whole pool rolled down to the smaller prizes, so every $2 ticket carried at least $2.30 of prize money. A retired Michigan couple and a group of MIT students bought hundreds of thousands of tickets on those draws. Syndicates wagered about $40M and won about $48M.

      Closed byA 2011 Boston Globe investigation. The game ended in January 2012. Powerball and Mega Millions never roll down.
      2005–11

      Eddie Tipton rigs the random-number generatorsFraud

      The security director of the Multi-State Lottery Association hid code that made some games' draws predictable on a few dates each year. His brother and friends claimed prizes in Colorado, Wisconsin, Oklahoma and Kansas before a claim on an Iowa jackpot unraveled it.

      Closed byA 25-year sentence in 2017. The only known “edge” in one of that association's games was this crime.
      2023

      A syndicate buys out Lotto TexasMath

      Lotto Texas has 25,827,165 combinations at $1. Buyers backed by Australian gamblers covered more than 99% of them for about $25.8M, reportedly skipping the most popular combinations, and took the $57.8M cash jackpot.

      Closed byTexas made selling over 100 tickets in one transaction a crime and abolished its lottery commission in 2025. Arizona, New York, Idaho and Maine have added bulk-buying limits since.
      2023

      27 tickets that guarantee a winMath

      Manchester mathematicians David Cushing and David Stewart used finite geometry to find 27 UK Lotto tickets (£54) that always match at least two numbers, and proved 26 can't.

      The catchMatching two numbers wins a free £2 Lucky Dip. The expected loss on the set is about £28. Guaranteed to win is not guaranteed to profit.

      Why none of this works on Powerball or Mega Millions

      • Covering every combination costs $584M for Powerball and $1.45B for Mega Millions, and you'd still share the jackpot with everyone else who won.
      • There are no roll-downs. An unwon jackpot just grows.
      • Smaller prizes are fixed amounts outside California, so there is no pool to capture.
      • Ball machines are audited, and past numbers carry no information.
      • Buying huge blocks of tickets is now restricted in Texas, New York, Arizona, Idaho and Maine.

      Where a sliver of edge still exists

      • Crowd-avoiding numbers. The one lever on these games, worth a few percent of the jackpot's value at record sizes.
      • California's split prizes. Every California prize tier is shared among California winners. Over the last 118 Powerball draws its Match 4 paid anywhere from $241 to $865: a median of $376 when three or more winning numbers were 31 or lower, against $445 otherwise. There, unpopular numbers raise the small prizes too.
      • Scratch-off data. States like Texas publish how many prizes remain in each scratch game, and pricier games pay back more (75 to 80% on some $20 to $100 Texas tickets). A game near its end can rarely turn positive, but claimed-late prizes make the data look better than it is (Garibaldi, 2024).
      • Second-chance drawings. Many states let you enter losing tickets into free extra drawings. It adds cents per ticket, and it's free.
      After the win

      What happens to people who win

      Your questionWho were the winners before, and what happened to them after?

      Two kinds of evidence answer that. Large studies follow thousands of winners through tax, bankruptcy and health records, so they show what typically happens. Individual stories show the range, but they only reach the news when something goes very right or very wrong. Here are both.

      What typically happens: the big studies

      Life satisfaction
      Better

      Swedish winners were more satisfied with their lives 5 to 22 years after winning, with no sign of the effect fading. The biggest gain was in how they felt about their finances. The effect is modest per dollar: about 0.04 standard deviations per $100,000 won.

      Lindqvist, Östling & Cesarini (2020), Review of Economic Studies. 3,362 winners. Paper
      Happiness and mental health
      Studies disagree

      The largest study found no significant change in day-to-day happiness or mental health. Smaller UK studies found better mental wellbeing about two years after mid-sized wins. The famous 1978 study claiming winners were no happier than anyone else surveyed only 22 winners.

      Lindqvist et al. (2020); Gardner & Oswald (2007), Journal of Health Economics; Brickman, Coates & Janoff-Bulman (1978). Gardner & Oswald
      Health and death rates
      No clear change

      Swedish records of hospital stays and deaths showed no change for adult winners. An earlier, smaller Swedish study had found winners got healthier; the larger study did not reproduce that.

      Cesarini, Lindqvist, Östling & Wallace (2016), Quarterly Journal of Economics; Lindahl (2005), Journal of Human Resources. Paper
      Smoking and drinking
      Worse

      UK winners' mental health improved, but smokers smoked more and social drinking went up.

      Apouey & Clark (2015), Health Economics. Paper
      Social life
      Better

      In UK data, bigger wins were linked to meeting friends more often, driven mainly by the largest wins.

      Costa-Font & Powdthavee (2021, IZA discussion paper). Paper
      Work
      Neutral shift

      Winners earn somewhat less from work. In US tax records of 90,731 winners, earnings fell about $2.30 a year for every $100 won. In Sweden the drop was about 1.1% of the prize a year, lasting more than a decade.

      Golosov, Graber, Mogstad & Novgorodsky (2024), Quarterly Journal of Economics; Cesarini, Lindqvist, Notowidigdo & Östling (2017), American Economic Review. US studySwedish study
      Spending and saving
      Neutral shift

      The money is mostly spread over years rather than blown at once. US winners saved most of the windfall. Norwegian winners' spending peaked in the year of the win and was back to normal within about five years. Swedish winners still had extra wealth more than a decade later, and people who had never owned stocks became more likely to.

      Golosov et al. (2024); Fagereng, Holm & Natvik (2021), AEJ: Macroeconomics; Cesarini et al. (2016); Briggs et al. (2021), Journal of Financial Economics. Norway study
      Bankruptcy
      Delayed, not prevented

      Florida winners of $50,000 to $150,000 were less likely to go bankrupt right after winning, but a few years later they were as likely as people who won small prizes. No study has measured the share of jackpot winners who go bankrupt.

      Hankins, Hoekstra & Skiba (2011), Review of Economics and Statistics. Abstract
      Neighbors
      Worse

      Winners' neighbors change too. Dutch winners' next-door neighbors were more likely to buy a new car. In Canada, a win worth a year's median income raised close neighbors' bankruptcy rate by about 7%.

      Kuhn, Kooreman, Soetevent & Kapteyn (2011), American Economic Review; Agarwal, Mikhed & Scholnick (2020), Review of Financial Studies. Dutch studyCanadian study
      Marriage and divorce
      Studies disagree

      An early Florida study found wins of $25,000 to $50,000 didn't change divorce rates but reduced marriage among single women. Newer, larger studies from the US, Sweden and Taiwan find slightly more marriage after a win, mainly among men, and US tax data show slightly less divorce.

      Hankins & Hoekstra (2011), Journal of Human Resources; Golosov et al. (2024); Cesarini, Lindqvist, Östling & Terskaya (2023, working paper). Newer study
      Children
      Mostly no change

      Swedish winners' children did no better in school and had slightly more hospital stays. In the US, college enrollment rose a little, and more after wins over $1M. Descendants of the winners of Georgia's 1832 land lottery did no better than others.

      Cesarini et al. (2016); Bulman, Fairlie, Goodman & Isen (2021), American Economic Review; Bleakley & Ferrie (2016), Quarterly Journal of Economics. US study
      Politics
      Neutral shift

      Winners become more opposed to estate and wealth taxes and drift slightly to the right. Voter turnout doesn't change.

      Doherty, Gerber & Green (2006), Political Psychology; Powdthavee & Oswald (2014); Brännlund et al. (2024, working paper). Swedish study

      Most of these studies cover prizes of about $10,000 to $1M, not giant jackpots. Several come from Scandinavia, where health care and safety nets are strong, so effects elsewhere may be larger.

      70%

      “70% of lottery winners go broke” has no source. It is usually credited to the National Endowment for Financial Education, which said in 2018 that the figure isn't its research and can't be confirmed. It was probably said by a participant at a 2001 meeting and repeated ever since. The “one-third go bankrupt” claim has no traceable source either. NEFE statement

      The honest pitch

      What AI is actually good for here

      This page was built the way you suggested: an AI crunching public data. That is how it found the real, small levers above, and why it can say with confidence that there is no big one.

      Useful

      • Doing the expected-value math properly: cash value, taxes, every prize tier and the odds of splitting.
      • Reading public data at scale. The popularity model here came from the prize-winner counts of 2,327 draws.
      • Generating tickets that avoid the crowd and don't overlap.
      • Checking your favorite numbers for patterns other people also play.
      • Ranking scratch-off games by the prizes left, where a state publishes it.
      • Telling you when not to play, which is almost always.

      Not possible

      • Predicting winning numbers. The balls have no memory, and 2,345 draws show no usable bias.
      • Finding patterns in past results. In random data, every pattern is noise, and models that claim otherwise are overfitting.
      • Changing the house edge. Half of every dollar never comes back as prizes, and taxes come out of what does.
      • Making a Powerball or Mega Millions ticket worth its price, even at the $2.04B record.
      • Guaranteeing a profit. “Guaranteed win” systems guarantee small prizes at a loss.
      Questions

      FAQ

      Are quick picks worse than choosing my own numbers?

      No. Every combination has exactly the same odds. Quick picks are slightly better than typical self-picks, because they are random and don't pile onto birthdays. Only deliberately unpopular picks beat them, and only by splitting less.

      Should I avoid numbers that just came up?

      It makes no difference to your odds. The machine doesn't remember the last draw. Some players avoid recent numbers and some replay whole winning sets, so exact past winners are worth avoiding for splitting reasons only.

      Do wheeling systems work?

      A wheel arranges numbers so that if some of them are drawn, you're guaranteed a small prize. It doesn't change the value of any ticket or the jackpot odds per ticket. The spread builder above does the useful part for free.

      Does buying more tickets improve my odds?

      Yes, in direct proportion, and your expected loss grows in the same proportion. 100 Powerball tickets are 100 chances in 292 million, and at tonight's jackpot they lose about $137 on average.

      Is Power Play worth it?

      Per dollar, the $1 Power Play returns about 41 cents after tax, a little more than the base ticket's 32 cents at tonight's jackpot, because it only multiplies smaller prizes that are taxed lightly. It still loses 59 cents of every dollar. Mega Millions has no add-on: its $5 price includes a random 2× to 10× multiplier that averages exactly 3×.

      Why does Mega Millions look so different?

      Since April 2025 it costs $5 a play, and sales collapsed: 5 to 25 million plays a draw, against 8 to 280 million for Powerball. Splits are rare, but the price is so high that even the $1.6B record jackpot, with no taxes and no splitting, would make a ticket worth about $3.85.

      Should a pool or office syndicate play?

      A pool doesn't change the value per dollar. It trades a tiny chance at a huge prize for a bigger chance at a smaller one. If you do it, write down who paid for which tickets before the draw.

      What about the woman who won four big Texas prizes?

      Joan Ginther won about $20M across four Texas prizes between 1993 and 2010. A records-based Philadelphia Inquirer investigation found she claimed 28 prizes from $1,000 to $10M after buying enormous numbers of expensive scratch tickets, and found no evidence of inside information. Her doctorate was in mathematics education, not statistics.

      Lump sum or annuity?

      They are worth about the same today. The advertised jackpot is 30 payments that grow 5% a year, and the cash option is what the lottery would spend on bonds to fund them, about 41% of the headline right now. That's why this page values jackpots at the cash option.

      So is it ever rational to play?

      As entertainment with money set aside for fun, sure. As a way to make money, no. After tax a ticket returns roughly 30 to 45 cents on the dollar, and the only math that ever beat a lottery needed a flawed game and millions of dollars.

      How the numbers are made

      Ticket value uses the cash option, 37% federal tax plus state tax on prizes of $1M or more, 24% on prizes over $5,000, and no tax on smaller prizes, which flatters the ticket slightly. Splitting uses a Poisson model: with λ other copies of your ticket expected, your average share is (1 − e−λ)/λ. Expected ticket sales at each jackpot size come from the median sales of past draws at similar jackpots (Powerball since August 2021, Mega Millions since the April 2025 format). The popularity model says each self-picked ticket's five numbers are chosen with probability proportional to the product of per-number weights, mixed with 70% uniform quick picks. It is fitted to all nine prize tiers of every draw by maximum likelihood, with each draw's ticket count estimated along the way. It captures single-number habits, not famous patterns, which is why the crowd check flags patterns separately. All figures are as of September 30, 2026.
